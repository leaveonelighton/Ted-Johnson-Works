Who Moved My Cheese? What Every Generation Should Know About Retirement

Why older Americans are returning to work—and what younger generations can learn before it's their turn.

By Ted Johnson · November 21, 2026

Retirement used to sound pretty simple. Work hard. Put in your years. Save some money. Retire. Enjoy life.

But what happens when the plan changes? What happens when prices rise, savings fall short, or you discover you're simply not ready to stop working?

Somebody moved the cheese.

And for many Americans, retirement isn't the final chapter they expected.

Why are older Americans returning to work?

In AARP research updated in August 2026, 6% of surveyed retirees reported returning to work in the previous six months. Among these returnees, 48% identified economic need as their primary motivation; 18% primarily cited boredom or a desire to stay active.

Some people return because they need income for everyday expenses. Others enjoy contributing, learning, or being around people. We shouldn't assume we know someone's circumstances simply because we see an older person working.

Maybe they need the paycheck. Maybe they enjoy the work. Maybe it's both. Maybe the full story is theirs to tell, not ours to guess.

The younger generation needs to hear this

If you're 25, 30, or 40 years old, retirement may seem like something to think about later. You're already dealing with groceries, housing, family, debt and everyday life.

But the Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey, published in May 2026, found that only 22% of non-retired adults ages 18–29 felt their retirement savings plans were on track. Across all non-retired adults surveyed, the figure was 35%.

That doesn't mean young people aren't trying. It means preparation matters, even when money is tight.

Learn what your workplace retirement plan offers. Understand any employer matching contribution. Try to build emergency savings over time. Keep an eye on debt. And remember: the work you do today may not look the same decades from now.

Age is not the end of opportunity

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, 19.1% of Americans ages 65 and older were working or actively seeking work in 2025—nearly one in five.

Older workers can bring experience and judgment developed over decades. Younger workers bring new ideas and different skills. We need both.

Age discrimination is a real challenge. In AARP research updated July 2026, 64% of surveyed workers age 50-plus said they had seen or experienced age discrimination in the workplace.

Let's not assume someone can't learn or contribute because of their age. And let's not assume an experienced worker has nothing to learn from someone who just started.

The cheese moved. So did Ted!

After nearly 30 years in technology, I found myself returning to Amazon in a different role. I'm grateful for the opportunity.

And yes, there's something funny about being an author whose books are sold on Amazon while also working at Amazon. Life has a sense of humor!

I'm still learning, working, writing and adapting. This isn't a story about having every answer. It's about recognizing that life doesn't always follow our original plan.

Starting something different doesn't erase what we've accomplished. And it doesn't mean we've failed.

Five things worth learning before retirement

1. Start earlier than you think you need to. Even small, consistent savings can matter over time. Learn your options; don't assume you'll figure everything out later.

2. Build some financial flexibility. Emergency savings, manageable debt and a realistic budget can make an unexpected change less difficult.

3. Keep learning. Technology and work requirements will change. Staying curious is valuable at any age.

4. Pay attention to your health. Understand what a job actually requires physically and mentally. Protecting your health matters more than proving a point.

5. Don't judge somebody else's next chapter. They may be supporting family, rebuilding savings, seeking purpose or simply enjoying the work. Treat them with respect.
