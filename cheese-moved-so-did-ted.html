The Cheese Moved. So Did Ted.

Retired from IT, but Called Back to Active Duty!

By Ted Johnson · November 21, 2026 · Updated November 27, 2026

I spent decades solving technology problems. Today, I’m back working in Amazon operations in Cleburne, Texas. That was not the retirement picture I once had in mind.

“Called back to active duty” is my way of describing a return to work. It is a personal metaphor, not a claim of military service or an Amazon slogan.

I’m grateful for the opportunity. I also want to be honest about why I returned: I needed income. This is a seasonal job and a financial lifeline, with no promise of a permanent position or a return to IT.

The cheese moved. So did Ted. But adapting involves more than a positive attitude. Money, health, responsibilities and the kind of work available all matter.

A different door into familiar territory

My earlier path into Amazon started with applications for technology jobs that did not lead to an offer. An operations hiring event opened another door. At DAL9, I built pallets and later worked inbound, unloading trailers. During that earlier chapter I also served in an interim Safety Coordinator assignment, joined the TOM Team and recorded 888 moves. After more applications and interviews, I eventually spent more than two years in Amazon IT.

That history is my own account, not a hiring formula. Working hard and applying internally did not make advancement automatic. Today I am in a seasonal operations role at TXZ5 in Cleburne. The site code and city can be corroborated in a Texas environmental permitting document; I am making no claim that it is the world’s largest facility. [9]

There is an irony I enjoy: Amazon sells books I wrote while I am helping move packages. My experience still has value. I am also learning again.

The conversations behind this article

I have been speaking directly with other older workers who have experienced displacement and returned to employment. Those conversations are part of why I wanted to examine this subject more carefully.

They are firsthand perspective, not a representative survey. I am not assigning anyone a story, repeating private remarks or identifying coworkers without their consent. The experiences of people I meet cannot tell us how often something happens across America. They can remind us to ask better questions about the person standing beside us.

Someone starting over may bring years of experience that their current job title does not reveal. A younger colleague may have knowledge that makes the next task easier. Both deserve respect, useful training and a fair chance to contribute.

Choosing to work and needing to work are different

AARP’s August 2026 research update reported that 6% of surveyed retirees had returned to work within six months. Among returnees, 48% named economic need as their main reason; 18% primarily cited boredom or wanting to remain active. These are different denominators, and the returnee subgroup was small. The 6% was unchanged from summer 2025, so this finding does not establish a sudden surge. [1]

Some people can choose hours, tasks and an exit date. Others need the next paycheck and have fewer alternatives. A person can also want companionship and need income at the same time. Treating every return as an inspiring lifestyle choice misses that difference.

BLS reported 19.1% labor-force participation among people 65 and older in 2025, versus 20.2% in 2019. Participation includes people working or looking for work, not just retirees returning. The 2025 annual estimate excludes October because shutdown-related data were not collected. It cannot tell us why any particular person returned. [2]

Financial impact: what is left after the shift?

A paycheck can provide room to breathe. My practical question is how much remains after transportation, meals, required work expenses and any change in benefits. Seasonal income also needs a plan for the possibility that the assignment ends. A bigger gross paycheck alone does not answer whether an arrangement is sustainable.

Retirement uncertainty crosses generations. In the Federal Reserve’s 2025 survey, 22% of nonretirees ages 18–29 felt their retirement savings were on track, compared with 35% of nonretirees overall. That measures confidence, not actual retirement readiness. It gives families a reason to talk early without blaming younger adults for circumstances the survey does not explain. [3]

For someone claiming Social Security retirement benefits before full retirement age, earnings-test rules may affect current payments. Check the SSA’s explanation and your own benefits and tax situation rather than assuming every dollar of wages simply adds to available income. [8]

Physical impact: the job matters as much as the birthday

A long shift handling packages is a different proposition from a few hours of desk work. Standing, lifting, repetition, pace, temperature and time to recover belong in the decision. So do training, equipment and the ability to ask for help.

NIOSH emphasizes fitting work demands to a person’s abilities and resources. Older workers are not uniformly more injury-prone; on average they have fewer injuries, but recovery can take longer. Age alone is a poor substitute for understanding the actual person and task. [4]

A 2022 retirement-research working paper linked more demanding or hazardous occupations with earlier retirement and disability, while flexibility and autonomy were associated with working longer. It was observational and used occupation-level measures. It does not establish what any particular warehouse role will do to my health. [6]

My encouragement is to assess the real job, including recovery between shifts. Persisting should not mean hiding an injury or treating pain as proof of character. Less strenuous work may fit one person; a more physical role may fit another. Needing income does not erase either person’s limits.

Psychological impact: purpose can help, pressure can hurt

There is satisfaction in being useful, learning a process and finishing a shift with a team. There can also be frustration in starting below a former level of responsibility, uncertainty about temporary work and the pressure of having little financial choice. Those are possibilities to discuss, not diagnoses I am assigning to coworkers.

A 2009 Health and Retirement Study analysis associated work after career retirement with better physical outcomes than full retirement. Its mental-health findings depended on the work: the advantage appeared for career-related bridge jobs, not clearly for jobs in a different field. The cohort began at ages 51–61 in 1992 and was followed through 1998. Association is not proof that working causes better health, and this older study is not a forecast for today’s worker. [5]

For me, that distinction matters. Returning to operations after an IT career is not the same experience as continuing in a familiar technical role. We should ask whether a job provides a workable balance of income, control, learning and strain.

Social impact: connection does not require a paycheck

Work can offer routine and people to talk with. It can also consume time and energy that a family, friend or community needs. I do not want to romanticize either side.

A 2025 overview of 15 systematic reviews found that retirement’s effects on health and well-being varied, including beneficial, harmful and neutral findings. Socioeconomic circumstances mattered, and inconsistent definitions and selection effects limited conclusions. This challenges the idea that retirement is inherently unhealthy or employment is a universal remedy. [7]

My conclusion is practical: paid work is one possible source of connection. Family, volunteering, mentoring and community life can be others. Retiring, returning or being unable to work should never determine someone’s worth.

Five questions worth asking together

  1. What does this job need to solve? Immediate bills, longer-term income, structure, companionship or a different career path?
  2. Does the actual work fit? Review tasks, pace, schedule, commute, recovery and available support, rather than relying on a job title.
  3. What is the realistic financial result? Include costs, benefits and what happens when seasonal work ends.
  4. What would make learning easier? Ask about clear instructions, practice, feedback and training. Offer experience without assuming another generation has nothing to teach you.
  5. What is the next review point? Reassess the arrangement after experiencing it. Changing direction is allowed.

For younger readers: talk with the older people in your life about what work and retirement actually look like. For older readers: your experience still counts, and asking for help does not cancel it.

My story is still being written.

I do not know where this job will lead. I know why I showed up, what I have learned before and that I still have more to learn.

Education should help us understand one another. Encouragement should leave room for honest limits. Practical compassion starts when we see a person’s experience as more than the job they happen to be doing today.
