LEARN’N AS WE GO · EPISODE 4

Life Savings Gone. Here's Your Identity Monitoring Subscription.

The answer must come before the money disappears—not only after.

By Ted Johnson · Ted Johnson Works

Imagine working your whole life, saving a little at a time, and then hearing that someone has persuaded a family member to send much of that money to a stranger.

The family gets an apology. Maybe they get identity monitoring. Maybe someone offers another subscription.

But the account is still empty.

Where was the protection when someone needed it?

One answer now is worth more than an apology later.

This isn't a small problem

In its report released April 6, 2026, the FBI said its Internet Crime Complaint Center received more than one million complaints about internet crime in 2025, with nearly $21 billion in reported losses. Americans aged 60 and older reported approximately $7.7 billion in losses. Those are reported losses, not proof of how much was ultimately recovered.

The FTC's October 2026 report separately found approximately $3 billion in fraud losses reported by people aged 60 and over in 2025, up from roughly $600 million in 2020. These figures come from different reporting systems and must not be added together.

Older adults are not simply more gullible. The FTC notes that younger consumers often report losing money more frequently, while older adults can experience much larger individual losses, especially in investment, impersonation, and tech support schemes.

How the trap works

Someone calls, texts, or shows a frightening browser pop-up. They claim to be a bank employee, a government agent, or technical support. They say your accounts are at risk and you must move the money to protect it. The threat is invented; the urgency is real.

Others build trust gradually through romance or investment relationships. Some use cryptocurrency kiosks, wire transfers, payment applications, or cash. The actual technique changes, but the dangerous step is the same: the victim is persuaded to send money voluntarily to a criminal.

Never move money to a “safe account” at the direction of an unexpected caller or message. Independently call your bank using the number on your card or official statement—not the number they provide.

Identity monitoring: useful, but not enough

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warns that credit and identity monitoring don't necessarily stop identity theft before it happens. Monitoring may alert you that something changed in your credit file. It cannot reliably prevent a scammer from convincing you to authorize a transfer from an existing account.

A free credit freeze with the three major bureaus can make it harder to open new accounts in your name. That's useful protection, but it doesn't freeze your existing bank balance or block all types of fraud.

What could help before the money moves?

The CFPB and other financial regulators describe measures institutions can take against elder exploitation: trained staff, appropriate transaction holds, suspicious-activity review, and processes for trusted contacts. These safeguards must respect customer rights and applicable law; not every institution offers every option.

People can also ask their bank about transaction alerts, transfer limits, high-risk transfer verification, and a trusted-contact designation. A trusted contact should not automatically receive control of the account.

If money has just been sent: act now

  1. Stop contact with the scammer. Do not pay anyone who says they can unlock or recover the money for an upfront fee.
  2. Contact your bank, card issuer, payment app, or wire service immediately using a verified number. Report the fraudulent transaction and request a recall, reversal, or refund as appropriate. The sooner you act, the better the chance of intervention, but recovery is not guaranteed.
  3. Report online crime to FBI IC3 and the scam to FTC ReportFraud. Provide transaction details, account information needed by the official reporting forms, and relevant communications—never through an unsolicited recovery service.
  4. If personal information was exposed, use IdentityTheft.gov for a tailored recovery plan. Change affected passwords from a trusted device and review account security.
  5. Tell a trusted person. A second set of eyes can help interrupt further coercion. Save messages and receipts and ask your financial institution about next steps.

The FTC's scam response guidance explains different actions for bank transfers, cards, payment apps, gift cards, and exposed accounts. One recovery process doesn't fit every payment method.

A different measure of success

Maybe a bank should be measured not only by how efficiently money moves, but also by how quickly a suspicious transfer can be questioned and a customer can reach a human who understands the danger.

Maybe an identity service should explain clearly what it can prevent, what it can merely detect, and what it cannot do at all.

And maybe every person deserves a one-page action plan before a scammer calls.

The best fraud alert is the one that prevents a loss—not the one that documents it afterward.

Technology shouldn't leave humanity behind—and it shouldn't pass us by!
