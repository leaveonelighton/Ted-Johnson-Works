How Many Passwords Does One Human Need?

Passwords, passkeys, recovery codes—and a human being trying to remember it all.

By Ted Johnson · Ted Johnson Works

I've spent more than 30 years in information technology, and here's a question I keep coming back to: Who, exactly, is supposed to remember all these passwords?

We have bank logins, email accounts, medical portals, shopping sites, phones, business platforms, and recovery codes. One service wants a special character. Another wants a new password. Another insists on texting a phone number we no longer have.

It's easy to blame the customer. But maybe we've been asking the customer to do too much.

If being secure requires remembering a hundred secrets, perhaps the design is part of the problem.

What the current guidance actually says

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) updated its digital identity guidelines in 2025. For passwords used on their own, the standard requires at least 15 characters in systems adopting that rule. NIST rejects mandatory mixtures of uppercase, symbols, and numbers, and rejects routine forced password changes unless compromise is suspected.

NIST also calls for websites to allow password managers and autofill. A secure manager can generate and remember unique passwords so people don't have to. That's much better than reusing one password across your bank, email, and shopping accounts.

A crucial difference: Those are NIST guidelines for covered implementations; not every existing website follows them, and they don't mean a 15-character password alone is sufficient for every high-risk account.

Passkeys can remove some of the burden

A passkey uses cryptography instead of asking you to type a reusable secret into a website. On supported devices, a local fingerprint, face unlock, or device PIN can unlock the passkey. It is generally phishing-resistant because the credential is bound to the legitimate website.

But the FIDO Alliance doesn't eliminate another human problem: What happens when you lose the device, change your phone, or lose access to the account that syncs your passkeys? You still need a recovery plan.

A practical setup for ordinary people

  1. Start with your main email account. Protect it with a unique, strong password and multi-factor authentication or a passkey. Your email may be the recovery key to everything else.
  2. Use one reputable password manager. Let it generate unique passwords. Protect the manager itself with a strong, memorable master passphrase and a second factor where supported. Don't store its recovery secret only inside the manager.
  3. Enable passkeys where they work for you. Keep a second supported device, security key, or documented recovery route where possible.
  4. Save recovery codes offline. Store them somewhere private and secure, not in a public note, screenshot album, or message to a stranger.
  5. Review your financial accounts. Turn on transaction alerts and confirm that your phone number, email, and backup contact methods are current.
  6. Practice the recovery path. Know where to find account recovery instructions before you're locked out.

One thing no password can fix

Even perfect passwords won't stop a scammer who convinces a legitimate account holder to send money. That's why our next episode examines fraud prevention beyond identity monitoring.

Security shouldn't depend on how many passwords a person can remember. It should depend on how well the system protects them.

Technology shouldn't leave humanity behind—and it shouldn't pass us by!
