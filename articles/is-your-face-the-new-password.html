Is Your Face the New Password?

What happens when you're still you, but the computer says you aren't?

By Ted Johnson · Ted Johnson Works

Here's a question I never imagined asking when I started working in computers: What happens when a face-recognition system doesn't recognize the person who owns the account?

Imagine a person who's been through an accident, reconstructive surgery, a serious illness, or simply the normal changes that come with age. They're the same person. They have the same memories, responsibilities, family, and bank account.

But the camera looks at them and says: Not recognized.

Do they need a new face to get their old life back? That's a terrible joke, but it's also an important design question.

If your identity hasn't changed, why should your ability to prove it depend on your face staying the same?

Biometrics are convenient—but not magic

A biometric system compares a current measurement—such as a face or fingerprint—with a stored reference. It's a comparison with a threshold, not a magical declaration of personal identity. The result can be wrong.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) calls an incorrect rejection of the real person a false non-match. Its digital identity guidance explicitly discusses problems caused by facial changes, eye surgery, masks, and finger injuries. NIST also stresses that alternative non-biometric authentication should be available in systems designed to follow its guidance.

NIST's facial-recognition evaluations show that performance depends on the particular algorithm and the quality of the images. A system may work very well for one setting while still failing a real person in another.

Your face is not the same thing as a passkey

People sometimes mix up three different ideas: facial recognition used to identify someone, face unlock on a phone, and a passkey used to sign in to a website.

Here's the difference. A passkey uses public-key cryptography. Your device holds a private credential, and the website verifies a cryptographic response. Your fingerprint or face may be one convenient way of unlocking that credential locally—but on supported devices a PIN or device password can do the job instead.

According to the FIDO Alliance, biometric processing for FIDO passkeys remains on the device rather than sending your face or fingerprint to the website. That's an important privacy distinction.

Apple likewise explains that Face ID requires a device passcode and can accommodate an alternative appearance. Exactly what recovery choices are available still depends on the device, service, and how the account was set up.

When technology gets it wrong, who gets the last word?

Consider the customer who can't easily use a camera, the worker with a hand injury, or the person who has changed phones during a medical emergency. A good security design plans for these cases before somebody is locked out.

A backup login method isn't a weakness just because it isn't biometric. The goal is to offer an alternative that's both usable and appropriately secure, with safeguards against impersonation and social engineering.

It's not enough to tell somebody to contact support. The route back in should be clear, safe, accessible, and proportionate to what the person is trying to do.

And then there's AI

AI can help recognize suspicious activity, but convincing synthetic pictures and videos also make identity checks more complicated. That is a reason for layered security—not a reason to believe that every camera can be trusted or that every person should be forced to use biometrics.

Security that locks out the rightful owner has a human cost. Security that admits the wrong person has a financial and privacy cost. Responsible design must consider both.

The question isn't just whether a computer can recognize your face. It's whether technology can recognize your humanity.

Here's what I'd ask every company building these systems

  • What happens if facial recognition fails—or the customer's appearance changes permanently?
  • Is there an accessible, genuinely secure way to sign in without biometrics?
  • Can people recover their accounts if they lose or replace a device?
  • Does the customer know what information is stored, where, and for how long?
  • Are false rejections and recovery delays measured as seriously as fraudulent sign-ins?

I'm not against biometric technology. I'm against forgetting the people it's supposed to serve.

And before someone suggests increasingly creative body parts for identification—let's make sure the privacy policy is readable first! 😂

What do you think? Would you trust your face to unlock everything you own, provided there's a secure alternative when it doesn't work?

Technology shouldn't leave humanity behind—and it shouldn't pass us by!
