Working under Chef Al Hynes at the Belleview-Biltmore.

Pastry, production, discipline, and one particular day when an ordinary hotel shift put me in a room full of Secret Service agents.

Belleair, Florida · Culinary work · 1980s recollection

Long before I wrote books or built Ted Johnson Works, I spent part of my working life in one of the most unusual classrooms I could have asked for: the kitchen of Florida's historic Belleview-Biltmore Hotel.

I worked under Chef Al Hynes and learned by doing. The work was fast, repetitive, exacting, and real. Morning danishes. Hard rolls. Key-lime pies. Production prep. Enough eggs that I can still remember separating what felt like an endless supply of them.

That kind of kitchen teaches lessons that do not fit neatly into a classroom.

Preparation makes speed possible.

When guests are waiting, the clock does not care how you feel that morning. The work has to be ready. You learn organization, timing, consistency, and the value of doing the same thing correctly more than once.

You also learn that "good enough" changes when other people are depending on your part of the operation.

The lesson I carried forward:

Learn the work. Do the work. Pay attention. Get better. Help somebody with what you learned.

Chef Hynes was part of a real seasonal hotel tradition.

Years later I found an independent account from another cook who trained under Chef Al Hynes. He describes working summers with Hynes at Chatham Bars Inn on Cape Cod and winters at the Belleview-Biltmore in Belleair. That immediately sounded familiar to me because it matches the seasonal hotel world I remember.

For me, Chef Hynes was not an abstract name on a résumé. He was part of a working kitchen where standards mattered and where you learned by producing food people were actually going to eat.

Then there was one particular day at work.

One day in the 1980s, during George H. W. Bush's vice-presidential years (1981-1989), I remember a visit or event associated with him at the Belleview-Biltmore. I was working in the employee cafe and served roughly 30 Secret Service personnel.

I do not remember the exact date, and I am not going to invent one. I present that part exactly for what it is: my firsthand recollection.

What makes the memory especially interesting today is that the hotel's documented guest history does include George H. W. Bush, and other published Belleview accounts describe substantial Secret Service presence at the property during high-profile visits.

At the time, though, none of that felt like a history lesson.

It felt like work.

Feed the people in front of you. Keep the operation moving. Do your job.

The education included my mistakes too.

I was young, and I did not always make great decisions. One of those decisions eventually cost me the job after I overslept following a night of drinking.

That belongs in the story because a real education includes more than the things that make you look good. Sometimes the lesson arrives because you did something wrong and had to live with the result.

I can see the thread now.

Food, technology, teaching, fitness, writing, service - they looked like separate chapters when I was living them.

Looking back, the connection is much easier to see.

I have always learned best by getting close to the work, paying attention to people who knew what they were doing, practicing until something became useful, and then passing the lesson along.

The Belleview-Biltmore was one of the places where that pattern took shape.

I did not leave there as a famous chef. I left knowing how much work sits behind something that looks effortless when it reaches the customer.
